\chapter{Celestial coordinates}
\label{ch:coords}

\section{The celestial sphere}

Seen from the ground, the night sky behaves like a sphere turning from east to
west about an axis through the celestial poles. Stars lie at wildly different
distances, but their position on that fictitious sphere is all one needs to
find and follow them.

The Earth completes one turn in \textbf{23~h 56~min 4~s} --- the
\emph{sidereal day}, shorter than the 24 hours of the solar day. That, and not
the clock, is the rate a mount must turn at.

\section{Right ascension}

\textbf{Right ascension} (RA) is the celestial equivalent of longitude. It is
counted in \textbf{hours} rather than degrees, from 0~h to 24~h, starting at
the vernal point.

\begin{formula}
\[
1\,\text{h} = 15\degre \qquad 1\,\text{min} = 15' \qquad 1\,\text{s} = 15''
\]
So RA = 02~h 44~min 08~s is $2\times15\degre + 44\times0.25\degre +
8\times\frac{15}{3600}\degre = 41.03\degre$.
\end{formula}

\begin{info}
RA is the critical coordinate for tracking: a one-second timing error is worth
15 arcseconds on the sky at the celestial equator. At 1.6\arcsec/pixel that is
about ten pixels of trailing.
\end{info}

\section{Declination}

\textbf{Declination} (DEC) is the equivalent of latitude: the angular distance
north or south of the celestial equator, from $-90\degre$ to $+90\degre$. On a
correctly aligned mount the declination axis does not move while tracking.

\section{Hour angle}

The \textbf{hour angle} measures how far an object stands from the local
meridian. It grows continuously as the Earth turns.

\begin{formula}
\[
\text{HA} = \text{local sidereal time} - \text{RA}
\]
An RA tolerance can be expressed in arcseconds or in seconds of time. The
conversion depends on declination:
\[
\text{seconds of time} = \frac{\text{arcseconds}}{15 \cos(\text{DEC})}
\]
\end{formula}

\begin{tip}
Near the celestial pole an RA error barely moves the star on the sensor: the
cosine of the declination damps it. Circumpolar objects are therefore more
forgiving of RA tracking errors --- but not of declination ones.
\end{tip}

\chapter{Equatorial mounts}
\label{ch:mounts}

\section{Principle}

An \textbf{equatorial mount} cancels the Earth's rotation by turning the tube
about an axis parallel to the Earth's own: the polar axis.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Polar axis (RA)}: turns at the sidereal rate.
  \item \textbf{Declination axis}: still while tracking, in theory.
\end{itemize}

\begin{formula}
\[
\omega = \frac{1\,296\,000''}{86\,164.09\,\text{s}} \approx
15.041\,\text{arcseconds per second}
\]
\end{formula}

Any departure from that rate, and any misalignment of the polar axis, becomes a
drift the image records.

\section{10Micron mounts}

10Micron mounts (GM1000 HPS and above) carry \textbf{absolute encoders}: they
know where they point even after a power cut, where a relative encoder loses
it. To that they add a worm drive, periodic-error correction and, above all, an
internal \textbf{pointing model} built from several dozen stars.

That model is what makes \textbf{unguided} imaging possible: the mount does not
merely turn at the sidereal rate, it applies corrected rates that absorb
flexure, refraction and the residual polar misalignment.

\begin{warning}
A direct consequence, often misunderstood: on a modelled mount the
\textbf{polar alignment error lives inside the model} and is already
compensated by the tracking rates. A residual declination drift therefore does
not call for the azimuth screw, but for \textbf{more model points near the
target}.
\end{warning}

These mounts speak an \textbf{extended LX200}: beyond the standard commands
they expose raw axis positions, applied rates, model state and environmental
data. MountMonitor uses those extensions where available.

\chapter{What degrades tracking}
\label{ch:errors}

No mount tracks perfectly. The causes overlap, and each leaves a different
signature in the record.

\section{Periodic error}

A worm is never perfectly round nor perfectly centred. Its defect repeats with
every turn and produces an oscillation of fixed period --- a few minutes, most
often. That is exactly what a Fourier analysis can pull out
(chapter~\ref{ch:fft}).

\section{Polar alignment}

If the polar axis does not point exactly at the celestial pole, the star drifts
slowly in declination, the faster the further one is from the meridian. On a
mount without a model the answer is mechanical. On a modelled mount, see the
warning in the previous chapter.

\section{Atmospheric refraction}

The atmosphere lifts objects the more they are low: some $34'$ at the horizon,
$1'$ at $45\degre$ of altitude. Refraction therefore changes through the night,
and its drift is easily mistaken for a misalignment.

\section{Mechanical disturbance and vibration}

Wind, a cable pulling, ground movement, a meridian flip: so many one-off jolts,
none of them periodic. They show as isolated spikes, and that is what tells
them apart.

\section{Commanded moves}

These are not errors at all. Between exposures the sequencer deliberately moves
the mount --- \textbf{dither} to decorrelate noise, \textbf{re-centering} when
the target has slipped --- and the mount \emph{stays} at its new position.
Confusing them with tracking error is the costliest mistake one can make
reading a record, and chapter~\ref{ch:stats} is devoted to it.

\chapter{Reading the numbers}
\label{ch:stats}

\begin{warning}
This is the most important chapter in the manual. A tracking record is read
wrongly with disconcerting ease, and the figure that comes out is then wrong by
a factor of several hundred.
\end{warning}

\section{The signal is a staircase}

Here is what a night of unguided imaging actually records: the mount holds its
position to a fraction of an arcsecond, then the sequencer dithers it, and it
holds its new position just as well. The trace is not a noisy line about a
mean: it is a \textbf{staircase}.

A standard deviation taken over the whole night then measures the height of the
steps, not the quality of the tracking. And removing a straight line --- what a
classic detrending does --- does not remove a staircase.

\begin{info}
On a real eight-and-a-half hour session the raw combined RMS came out at
\textbf{86.3\arcsec}, a damning verdict. The mount was in fact holding each
position to \textbf{0.18\arcsec}. The two figures differ by a factor of 480.
\end{info}

\section{Jitter}

\textbf{Jitter} is the spread the mount shows \emph{while} it holds a position.
It is the only figure that smears a star on the sensor, and the one
MountMonitor bases its rating on.

It is measured between repositionings, never about a straight line. The program
cuts the record at the steps, drops two samples after each --- the move itself
is not tracking --- and takes the median of the per-step spreads.

\begin{tip}
This approach has a property worth noting: a sequence that dithers on every
frame does not come out worse than one that never does. The figure describes
the mount, not the sequencer.
\end{tip}

\section{Commanded moves}

They are counted and classified separately:

\begin{center}
\rowcolors{2}{rowalt}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lX}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{What it is} \\
\midrule
Dither & A small move between exposures, to decorrelate noise. \\
Re-centering & A larger move, bringing the target back where it belongs. \\
Unsettled & The spread that follows stays several times the usual value. That
is the one worth looking at. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{warning}
A threshold crossing is not a move. A 70\arcsec{} re-centering takes several
samples to complete and trips the threshold at each one: one night read as 98
crossings for 53 actual moves, with a median amplitude ten times too small.
MountMonitor groups crossings less than ten seconds apart.
\end{warning}

\section{Slow drift}

It is quoted per hour and per exposure. On an unguided mount every dither
cancels it: what matters is not the drift over the night, but the displacement
it produces \emph{during one exposure}.

\begin{formula}
\[
\text{displacement over one exposure} = \text{drift (\arcsec/h)} \times
\frac{\text{exposure (s)}}{3600}
\]
A drift of $-16.8\arcsec$/h therefore moves the star by $0.84\arcsec$ over a
180~s exposure.
\end{formula}

\section{Running standard deviation}

Computed live over a 60 to 900 second window, it serves to see \emph{when}
something happened, not to grade the night. After a target change, an
acquisition gap or a repositioning, its window straddles the move and describes
that rather than the tracking: those values are excluded from the report.

\section{The rating}

It rests on the combined jitter, with thresholds suited to the type of mount.
Beyond the last threshold the rating is POOR. The thresholds apply to the
jitter alone: drift and commanded moves do not enter it, which is why they are
tight even for an unguided mount.

\begin{center}
\rowcolors{2}{rowalt}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXX}
\toprule
\textbf{Rating} & \textbf{Guided mount} & \textbf{Unguided precision} \\
\midrule
Excellent & $< 0.4\arcsec$ & $< 0.5\arcsec$ \\
Good      & $< 1.0\arcsec$ & $< 1.0\arcsec$ \\
Fair      & $< 2.0\arcsec$ & $< 2.0\arcsec$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\begin{tip}
Always relate the rating to your sampling. At 1.6\arcsec/pixel, a
0.5\arcsec{} jitter shakes the star by a third of a pixel: invisible on the
final image.
\end{tip}

\chapter{Fourier analysis}
\label{ch:fft}

\section{What it is for}

A Fourier transform breaks a signal into a sum of sinusoids. On a tracking
record it brings out what \emph{repeats} --- and a worm periodic error repeats
by construction.

MountMonitor looks in the \textbf{2 to 15 minute} band, where the worm periods
of common mounts fall.

\section{Two precautions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The transform runs on \textbf{detrended} deviations. A linear drift is
  not periodic, but over a finite window it produces a large low-frequency
  component that would hide what is being looked for.
  \item The \textbf{Nyquist limit} requires sampling at least twice per period.
  At 2~Hz, periods below one second are out of reach --- no real inconvenience,
  no mechanical error lives there.
\end{itemize}

\section{Reading a spectrum}

A sharp peak at a stable period marks a periodic error. A flat spectrum means
there is no detectable one --- the case of 10Micron mounts after correction.
Broad, wandering peaks are noise, usually wind.

The FFT window is described in chapter~\ref{ch:fftwin}.

\chapter{What MountMonitor is for}
\label{ch:purpose}

MountMonitor watches an equatorial mount through the night while other
software (NINA, a sequencer, a guiding program) drives it. It never commands
the mount: it \emph{reads}. Twice a second it asks the mount for its position
and its time, and draws four things from them.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tracking quality.} How much the position moves while the mount
  is meant to hold a star: that is what smears the star on the sensor
  (chapter~\ref{ch:stats}).
  \item \textbf{The mount's settings.} Refraction, dual-axis tracking, tracking
  rate, follow object, tracking correction, GPS: a setting left wrong the day
  before spoils a whole night without anything showing on screen. MountMonitor
  reads them again at connection, after every slew and every five minutes, and
  opens an alert window when one of them is not the expected one.
  \item \textbf{Time.} The offset between the PC's clock, the mount's and a time
  server (NTP). A drifting clock spoils pointing and the time stamps of the
  frames.
  \item \textbf{Vibration}, if a seismometer is connected, and \textbf{the air}
  (temperature and pressure) the mount uses to correct refraction.
\end{enumerate}

Everything is recorded, and a \textbf{night report} is written on its own at
the end of the session, in the three languages (section~\ref{sec:report}).

\section{Reading it in ten seconds}

\begin{center}
\rowcolors{2}{rowalt}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lXX}
\toprule
\textbf{Where to look} & \textbf{All is well} & \textbf{Something is wrong} \\
\midrule
Status dot (top right) & Green, ``The mount is tracking'' & Grey (parked, idle),
orange (slewing) or red (error) \\
RA and DEC curves & A narrow band between the green tolerance lines & Repeated
excursions beyond the green lines, a ramp, regular oscillations \\
Blue $\sigma$ curve & Low and flat & Rising and staying high \\
Alert window & None & A ``Mount alert'' window in front \\
History & Lines with \checkmark{} after ``\#\#\# MOUNT SETTINGS CHECK \#\#\#'' &
Lines preceded by a warning triangle \\
Night report & Rating EXCELLENT or GOOD & FAIR or POOR, recommendations \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Chapter~\ref{ch:night} says what to do in each case.

\section{Supported systems}

The published packages carry their own Python and their own Qt: nothing else
needs installing. They do, however, require a fairly recent system, because the
Qt binaries they contain require it.

\begin{warning}
Pick the macOS package that matches your machine: \texttt{-macos-arm64.dmg}
for an Apple Silicon Mac, \texttt{-macos-x86\_64.dmg} for an Intel Mac. The
bundled Python is tied to one architecture even though Qt is universal: the
wrong package will not start at all.
\end{warning}

\section{Installing}

On Windows the installer does everything. On macOS, drag the application into
the Applications folder. On Linux, \texttt{installer.sh} installs it in the
user's profile, without \texttt{sudo} or a package manager.

Updates are checked quietly at every start and offered when there are any;
they are signed, and an archive whose signature does not match is refused.

\section{A first run without a mount}

Simulation mode lets you go through everything without hardware; it made the
screenshots of this manual:

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
MountMonitor --sim-all
\end{lstlisting}

\section{The strip of figures above the graphs}
\label{sec:strip}

Above each graph a line gives its current figures, each in the colour of what
it describes. It never covers the curves.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RA and DEC}: the current deviation, its standard deviation
  $\sigma$ over what is shown, in green the tolerance (``tol.~±0.100~s''), the
  session's minimum and maximum, in blue the largest running $\sigma$.
  \item \textbf{TIME}: in white the PC~--~mount offset and its drift per hour,
  in green the PC loop, in red the mount loop, in grey their mean, in blue the
  PC~--~NTP offset. These colours are also those of the curves.
\end{itemize}

\section{The RIGHT ASCENSION and DECLINATION graphs}
\label{sec:radec}

Each point is the deviation between the position read and the reference
position (by default the median of what is shown). In right ascension the unit
is the second of time (s) or the arcsecond (\arcsec), depending on the
\emph{right ascension in degrees} setting; in declination, the arcsecond.

\begin{center}
\rowcolors{2}{rowalt}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lX}
\toprule
\textbf{Line} & \textbf{What it shows} \\
\midrule
Magenta (RA) or red (DEC) curve & The measured deviation. Rounded without ever
going beyond the samples; on a short span, dots mark the real samples. Grey
when the mount is not tracking. \\
Blue curve, right-hand scale & The running standard deviation $\sigma$, over the
running window (60~s by default). It tells whether the mount \emph{holds} its
position, wherever it holds it. \\
Green lines & The tolerance, either side of the median. \\
Dashed lines in the curve's colour & The session's minimum and maximum. \\
Dashed blue line & The session's largest running $\sigma$. \\
Grey dash-dot line & The median, reference of the deviations. \\
Dotted grey vertical lines & Every 30~s, with the mount's time: time marks. \\

\textbf{Vertical scale} (View menu or right click): \emph{Data only} frames
the curve and lets the tolerance lines go out of view when tracking is much
better than they are; \emph{Tolerance} frames the tolerance; \emph{Maximum}
shows data and tolerance; \emph{Min/max lines} frames the minimum and maximum.
The $\sigma$ scale always starts at zero and rises to the power of ten above
3.3 times the largest $\sigma$, as in MountMonitor~3.

\begin{tip}
With the mount parked, right ascension drifts with the sky (15\arcsec{} per
second): the RA curve becomes a ramp. It is then greyed and the deviation is no
longer compared with the tolerance: this is not an error.
\end{tip}

\section{The TIME graph}
\label{sec:time}

It compares three clocks. The left axis is in milliseconds; the right axis, in
green, is for the loop times.

\begin{center}
\rowcolors{2}{rowalt}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{lX}
\toprule
\textbf{Curve} & \textbf{What it shows} \\
\midrule
White, PC -- mount & PC time minus mount time. A flat line is healthy; a slope
is a clock drift, given in ms/h in the strip above. \\
Green, PC loop & Time between two time answers from the mount, seen from the
PC. \\
Red, mount loop & The same interval, seen from the mount's clock. \\
Blue, PC -- NTP & PC time minus the time server's, when NTP is enabled. \\
Dashed grey line & Zero. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

Both loops are drawn centred on their mean, as in MountMonitor~3: what you read
is their regularity, not their value. A loop that grows longer and irregular
points to an overloaded PC or a slow link. With ASCOM the PC~--~mount offset
has no precise meaning: the driver answers from its cache, not from the
mount's clock.

\section{The SEISMIC graph}
\label{sec:seismic}

Shown when a seismometer is enabled (Preferences, Auxiliary). White curve: the
sensor's signal in ADC counts, re-centred by the offset setting; blue curve:
its running standard deviation; green lines: the seismic tolerance. A burst at
the same moment as RA or DEC excursions points to a vibration (a step, a door,
wind on the shelter), not to the mount.

\section{The axial graph}

Optional (Preferences, Processing, \emph{Axial mode}). It shows the speed or
the displacement of the mount's axes themselves, read with \texttt{:GaXa\#}
and \texttt{:GaXb\#} on a 10Micron (LX200, or ASCOM with the 10Micron
commands), computed as MountMonitor~3 does. \emph{Speed}: for each sample,
the slope of a straight line fitted to the axis angle over the running range,
drawn relative to its median; the strip gives the speed, the median and the
difference, in \arcsec/s (about 15.04 on the RA axis at the sidereal rate, 0
on the DEC axis, apart from polar error and refraction). The speed between
two samples is not drawn: the angle is read to 0.36\,\arcsec, which makes it
a hundred times noisier. \emph{Displacement}: the integral of (speed $-$
median) over the time shown, in \arcsec, drawn around its mean; the strip
gives the current value, the mean, and the peak-to-peak range, also on the
sky (times $\cos\delta$ for the RA axis, ``SPH'' in MountMonitor~3), to
compare with the pixel scale. With \emph{Align graphs on the running window},
the speed is moved back by half a range and the displacement by a whole one.
The first running range is not reliable yet.

\section{The status panel and the history}
\label{sec:panel}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dot and status}: green tracking, orange slewing, grey parked or
  idle, red error.
  \item \textbf{RA and DEC}: the position read, the deviation~$\Delta$ (red
  when beyond the tolerance, grey when the mount is not tracking) and the
  running $\sigma$.
  \item \textbf{Hz and samples}: the real sampling rate and the number of
  samples. With ASCOM the real rate is the driver's, about 0.5~Hz (see
  section~\ref{sec:conn}).
  \item \textbf{Air}: outside temperature and pressure read from the mount every
  30~s --- those it uses for refraction, whatever their source (weather station,
  script) ---, the temperature of the right ascension motor driver (warmer than the air) and the
  time until tracking stops (\texttt{:Gmte\#}, the mount's own estimate:
  meridian limit for the current pier side, or horizon limit). An arrow shows the direction of the last change.
\end{itemize}

The \textbf{history}, below, has the latest line at the top. It holds the
connection, the mount and its firmware, the site and the night's ephemeris, the
settings checks, the starts and ends of recording, and an air line whenever the
temperature changes by at least 0.5~°C or the pressure by at least 1~hPa.

\begin{warning}
\textbf{Only one program can open a serial port.} When NINA (or other
software) already drives the mount over its serial cable, choose
\textbf{ASCOM} with the \emph{same} driver: the 10Micron driver shares the one
link between all its clients. In LX200 serial MountMonitor would have to take
the port for itself, and one of the two programs would fail. Over the network
(LX200 TCP/IP) a 10Micron accepts several clients at once.
\end{warning}

With the 10Micron ASCOM driver 1.7 and \emph{10Micron commands through the
ASCOM driver} ticked, two particularities. The driver refuses the mount's
preference list (\texttt{:PRlist\#}) unless \emph{unchecked raw commands} are
allowed in its setup: until then follow object, tracking correction and
tracking speed are not checked, and the history says so. It also serves
positions from a cache refreshed about every two seconds: MountMonitor skips
the repeats, so the real rate is the driver's, and the history says so once.

\section{Unattended operation}
\label{sec:unattended}

For an observatory PC that starts everything at boot. These settings are in the
General tab and on a right click on the log button.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Connect to the mount when MountMonitor opens}, or start the
  program with \texttt{--connect}. At boot the mount's control box has often not
  finished starting: MountMonitor tries again every 30~s until the first
  connection, without opening any window.
  \item \textbf{Start recording when the mount starts tracking} (default). The
  button \emph{arms} the logger: nothing is written while the mount is not
  tracking. The button says what it is waiting for.
  \item \textbf{Suspend recording when the mount stops tracking} (default),
  after a two-minute grace delay: a meridian flip, an autofocus or a slew
  between targets does not cut the session. The file stays the same.
  \item \textbf{Night only} (default): record after sunset, end the night and
  write the report at sunrise. A mount that parks at 02:00 and resumes at 03:00
  stays one night, one file, one report. Unticked, for day-time sessions (the
  Sun), recording lasts until you stop it.
\end{itemize}

Files are forced to disk at least every 5~s: a power cut costs only a few
seconds.

\chapter{The mount checks}
\label{ch:checks}

They run at connection, after every slew, when the Preferences are accepted and
every 5~minutes. The results go into the history under the line ``\#\#\# MOUNT
SETTINGS CHECK \#\#\#''; the periodic check only writes what changed. The
rate-related checks only run while the mount is tracking.

\begin{center}
\rowcolors{2}{rowalt}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{p{3.3cm}Xp{4.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Check} & \textbf{What it verifies} & \textbf{If it is wrong} \\
\midrule
Refraction correction (\texttt{:GREF\#}) & That the mount corrects atmospheric
refraction. Nearly always wanted on. & Turn it on in the mount (keypad or the

Refraction update (\texttt{:WSG\#}) & Where temperature and pressure come

GPS (\texttt{:gtgpps\#}, \texttt{:gtg\#}) & That the mount's time is

When a setting is wrong, a single \textbf{alert window} opens in front, on the

\begin{tip}
An alert is not always a mount error: it says the mount does not match
\emph{your} expected values. If the mount is right (for instance ``not
updating'' because a script pushes the weather), it is the expected value that
must change, in the Miscellaneous tab.

\chapter{A night out}
\label{ch:night}

\section{How it goes}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Connect the mount; check the block of checks in the history (only
  \checkmark{} marks, or an alert to deal with).
  \item Arm the logger --- or let automatic arming do it.
  \item Let it run. Recording starts at the first tracking sample, pauses when
  the mount parks, and the report writes itself at daybreak.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What to watch live}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The running $\sigma$} (blue curve), more than the instantaneous
  deviation: it tells whether the mount holds its position.
  \item \textbf{Tolerance excursions}: the deviation~$\Delta$ turns red.
  \item \textbf{An alert window}: a mount setting has changed.
  \item \textbf{The white curve of the TIME graph}: it must stay flat.
\end{itemize}

\begin{tip}
An isolated spike is almost never the mount: it is a gust of wind, a cable, a
step too close to the pier. What matters is the level \emph{between} the
spikes.
\end{tip}

\section{When something is wrong}

\begin{center}

\toprule
\textbf{What you see} & \textbf{What it is, what to do} \\
\midrule

\needspace{0.6\textheight}
\section{The night report, section by section}
\label{sec:report}

\capture{en-rapport.png}

\begin{description}
  \item[Session overview] Date, observatory, mount, duration, number of samples
  (in all and while tracking), sampling rate, number of targets, file.

  \item[Overall quality] The \textbf{rating}, based on the \textbf{combined
  jitter} alone (see chapter~\ref{ch:stats}), then its three components:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Tracking jitter}: the spread while the mount holds a position,
    in RA, in DEC and combined. That is what smears the star.
    \item \emph{Commanded repositioning}: number of dithers and re-centerings,
    median size. No effect during an exposure.
    \item \emph{Slow drift}: in \arcsec/h, and the star motion it causes over a
    180~s exposure.
  \end{enumerate}
  \emph{For reference} gives the raw RMS (everything mixed) and with the drift
  removed: they are there to show why they are not used for the rating. Then
  the mount type, the rating thresholds and a one-line summary.

  \item[Target \#] For each target: its coordinates and number of samples; for
  RA then DEC, the jitter, the raw RMS, the \emph{reading resolution} (the
  smallest step the mount reports; a jitter close to this limit may be smaller in
  reality), the median running $\sigma$, the drift in \arcsec/min and \arcsec/h
  with its effect on one exposure and a verdict: no significant drift (under
  1\arcsec/h), slight (under 5), present but without effect on an exposure
  (under 15), significant beyond. Then the \emph{FFT} table of the dominant peaks
  (frequency, period, peak amplitude) and the periodic error verdict: detected if
  the main peak falls between 2 and 15~minutes, very low under 0.5\arcsec,
  acceptable under 1.5\arcsec, high beyond.

  \item[Time] (when a \texttt{.dti} exists) The PC~--~mount offset, its drift
  in ms/min (stable under 1~ms/min), the PC and mount loops (mean, maximum; a PC
  loop over one second means a struggling PC) and the PC~--~NTP offset (over
  one second, synchronise the clock).

  \item[Tracking status] Share of samples tracking, parked, idle, slewing. Under
  90~\% tracking deserves a look.

  \item[Commanded moves] Dithers, re-centerings and \emph{unsettled} moves
  (after which the spread stays several times its usual value: those deserve a
  look), median size, largest move, median duration and interval.

  \item[Night ephemeris] Sunset, nautical twilight, astronomical night,
  sunrise, and the share of the dark night the session actually covered.

  \item[Tolerance analysis] For thresholds from 0.5 to 5\arcsec, the share of
  samples whose jitter exceeds the threshold, on the same basis as the rating:
  between repositionings, drift removed.

  \item[Data continuity] Acquisition gaps (over five times the normal
  interval), the longest one, the total time lost.

  \item[Recommendations] What to do, target by target, when something calls
  for it.
\end{description}

\begin{tip}
A good night reads like this: rating EXCELLENT or GOOD, a jitter of a few
tenths of an arcsecond, drift ``without effect on an exposure'', no typical
periodic error, over 90~\% tracking, no recommendation.
\end{tip}

\section{Language}

The \textbf{Language} menu offers English, French and Dutch, plus automatic
detection from the system. Each entry is written in its own language: someone
who ends up in the wrong one cannot read the current one to find their way out.
A change takes effect at the next start.

\section{Problem reports}

If the program crashes, freezes or fails to start, it can report that by
itself. You are asked once, and nothing else is ever sent: not your tracking
data, not your file names, not your user name. All paths are anonymised.

In the \textbf{Help} menu, \textbf{Report a problem\ldots} sends a message
written by you to the same collection point, and the \textbf{Send crash
reports automatically} box withdraws or gives back consent at any time.
\textbf{Report a Bug on GitHub} opens a pre-filled issue on the project's
public page instead.
